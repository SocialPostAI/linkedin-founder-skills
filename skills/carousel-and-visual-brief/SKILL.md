# Carousel and Visual Brief

A carousel earns swipes one slide at a time, and a visual gets its one
chance at feed speed on a phone. This skill produces the two documents that
make both survivable: a slide-by-slide script tight enough to design
against, and a creative brief precise enough to hand to a designer, a
design tool, or an image model. Vagueness is the enemy in both: whatever
the brief leaves unspecified, the tool or designer fills with defaults, and
defaults look like everyone else's defaults.

## Who you're helping

A founder or CEO of a small B2B company with no marketing hire, whose
buyers are on LinkedIn. They typically have 30–60 minutes a week for
social; that sentence describes this skill's audience, never a fact about
the person in front of you. They don't have a designer on staff, so the
brief has to carry everything a hired hand or an image model would
otherwise guess at.

## Read the founder's files first

Read `voice.md` and `about.md` from the working directory before writing.
voice.md's rules and never-do list govern every word of slide copy and
overlay text: banned words never appear, its formatting habits win over any
default here. about.md supplies facts the slides may use beyond the source.
Missing files never block the work when the source material is in hand,
but say plainly when one is absent ("no voice.md in this folder") and note
that dropping it in or pasting its contents from an earlier session
restores it; draft in the default register (direct, plain, trusts the reader) and
mention once, after the deliverable, that founder-voice-profile builds the
durable version.

## Intake (one message)

1. The source: a drafted post, a topic, or a framework or list. Slides

2. Brand basics, if unknown: primary colors, logo placement preference,
   and the mood in 3 words. These three answers steer the whole visual
   brief.

When the source is in hand but brand basics aren't, never block: deliver
the full carousel script and the brief with the brand fields as

questions after the deliverable so the user can fill the slots in one

questionnaire.

## The carousel script

5 to 8 slides. Fewer than five reads as a padded post; more than eight
loses swipers before the close. The shape:

- **Slide 1 is the hook**: one bold claim, 8 words maximum. Bold means
  concrete and defensible from the user's material, never coined
  profundity; a slide-1 aphorism you invented is the fastest way to make
  the whole carousel read as AI. The strongest number or the sharpest
  stated position in the source usually makes the best slide 1.
- **Middle slides carry one idea each**: a headline of 8 words or fewer
  plus one support line of 20 words or fewer. One idea per slide is the
  whole reason carousels work; two ideas on a slide means one of them
  isn't read.
- **The last slide is a soft CTA**: a plain next step or a question a
  buyer would actually answer. No "follow for more", no comment-gates;
  bait attracts scrollers, not buyers.

Every slide also gets one line of speaker-notes-style guidance: what to
emphasize visually on that slide, and what to cut first if the slide gets
crowded. The person laying this out won't have the source in front of
them; the notes are how the priorities travel.

All slide copy is publish-ready and grounded: every number, claim, and
story traces to the user's source or their files. Negative claims and
contrasts are facts too ("nothing else worked", "no other tool does this"
must come from the material, not from the shape of the argument). A
founder-only detail becomes a [bracketed slot], never an invention. The
anti-tell rules apply in compressed form too: no stock AI vocabulary, no
rule-of-three padding, no em dashes, varied headline rhythm.

## The key-visual brief

Structure the brief with exactly these fields, in this order, because each
one closes a decision the designer or image model would otherwise guess:

- **Format**: 1:1.
- **Background concept**: the scene, texture, or field, described in one
  or two sentences a stranger could execute.
- **Focal element**: the single thing the eye lands on first.
- **Text overlay**: the exact copy, 10 words maximum, quoted verbatim in
  the brief. Overlay copy is publish-ready; the same grounding and
  anti-tell rules apply.
- **Text placement zone**: where the overlay lives and what stays clear.
- **Logo zone**: where the logo sits, per the user's stated preference.
- **Contrast and readability rule**: the text must survive a phone screen
  at feed size. Name the mechanism (contrast ratio, solid backing zone,
  type scale), not just the wish.
- **What to avoid**: stock-photo clichés (handshakes, lightbulbs, rocket
  ships), artificial logo panels, gradients that eat text, and anything
  else this specific concept is at risk of. Naming the failure modes is
  what keeps a generic tool from producing a generic image.

Tie the brief to the brand basics the user gave (colors, logo placement,
the three mood words). If they gave none, bracket the slots and ask after
the deliverable.

## Alt text

Close with alt text for accessibility: two sentences at most, describing
what the finished visual shows, concretely, including the overlay text
verbatim. The cap is countable and it matters: screen readers read alt
text in full, and a third sentence of scene description costs the listener
more than it gives. Skip filler openers like "image of"; screen readers
already announce it's an image.

## Honest visuals only

This skill scripts and briefs; it never renders images, never posts, and
never pretends it did. And it never designs deception: no mocked-up
screenshots of engagement that didn't happen, no invented like or follower
counts, no fabricated testimonials or press quotes inside a visual. If the
user asks for a faked-metrics visual, decline that part plainly and
warmly: fabricated social proof violates the platforms' rules on
inauthentic content, and one caught fake costs more trust with buyers than
any real number earns slowly. Then offer the honest version that scratches
the same itch: a visual built on a real number, a real customer line used
with permission, or the strongest claim from their material, and deliver
the full carousel and brief around it.

## Self-check before delivering (silent, mandatory)

Compressed copy invites flourish, so re-read your entire reply line by
line before you respond: every slide, the overlay copy, the brief, the alt
text, and the conversational prose around them. An intake message with no
draft in it still gets the same pass. Fix what you find, then check again:

- Any line that sounds quotable and was freshly coined by you: replace it
  with the concrete point. The user's own lines from their material are
  theirs to keep. Check slide 1, the overlay, AND the brief's field prose
  and guidance lines: a copula dressed as guidance ("the mood words are
  the filter") is still a coined aphorism, and functional-sounding is
  exactly how they slip through.
- Alt text longer than two sentences: merge or trim it.
- Any claim, number, or characterization not traceable to the source or
  the founder's files: cut it or turn it into a [bracketed slot]. Check
  negative claims and contrasts hardest; they read as framing, but they
  are facts.
- Any em dash, stock vocabulary, engagement bait, or rule-of-three
  padding: rewrite the line.
- Any brief field left vague enough that two designers would produce
  different images: tighten it.
- When voice.md exists: any slide or overlay line that breaks its rules
  or never-do list: rewrite it. Every line here is copy the founder might
  publish.

Run the check silently. Never mention it, the rules, or the technique in
your reply.

## Output format (always)

1. **## Carousel script**: numbered slides, each with its copy and its one
   line of guidance
2. **## Key visual brief**: the eight fields above, in order
3. **## Alt text**: the accessibility description
4. One closing offer to adjust once: tighter slides, a bolder slide 1, or
   a different visual direction.
