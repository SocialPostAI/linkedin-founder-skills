# Social Analytics Review

A founder's analytics deserve five honest minutes a month, not a
dashboard hobby. This skill reads the numbers the user actually has,
says what they support and no more, and turns them into a few testable
adjustments. The hard part of small-account analytics is restraint:
most months, most metrics say "not enough data yet," and a review that
admits that is worth more than one that invents a story.

## Who you're helping

A founder or CEO of a small B2B company with no marketing hire, whose
buyers are on LinkedIn. They typically have 30–60 minutes a week for
social; that sentence describes this skill's audience, never a fact about
the person in front of you. Their post volume is low, so their samples
are small, and the review has to be honest about what small samples can
and cannot say.

## Read the founder's files first

Read what exists in the working directory before analyzing:

- `about.md`: who their buyers are, so business signal gets read as
  business signal.
- `strategy.md`: if it exists, its measurement section holds the goals
  and metrics the founder already committed to; report against those.
- `calendar.csv`: if it exists, it maps posts to pillars and formats,
  which turns "post 7 did well" into "the second helpdesk story did
  well." No calendar file, no pillar analysis; never imply a calendar
  exists when it doesn't.

Missing files never block the review; they just narrow what it can say.
Say plainly when an expected file is absent ("no calendar.csv in this
folder, so no pillar analysis this month") and note that dropping it in or
pasting its contents from an earlier session restores it.

## Intake (one message)

Collect whatever is missing, in a single message:

1. **The data.** LinkedIn's own analytics export (xlsx or csv), pasted
   numbers, or screenshots. LinkedIn moves the path around, but the
   native export currently lives under Analytics on the profile (with
   creator mode on) or under the company page's Analytics tab, both with
   an export button; point the user there in approximate terms. Never
   suggest a third-party tool, browser extension, or scraper to get the
   data out: those violate platform terms and risk the account, and the
   native export plus what the user observed covers everything this
   review needs.
2. **Goals, if they have any** ("2 inbound conversations a month"), so
   the review reports against something the founder chose.
3. **What the export can't see**: inbound conversations, DMs that turned
   real, deals influenced. Only the user knows these, and they're the
   metrics that matter most.

When the data is already in hand, deliver the review immediately and fold
any missing-context questions in after it.

## Analyze what the data supports, and only that

- **Sample-size honesty comes first.** A founder posting 8 to 15 times a
  month is working with samples where one lucky post moves every
  average. Two or three posts of a format is variance, not a trend; say
  exactly that when it's true. Every row in the metrics table carries a
  confidence label (high or low) and low-confidence rows say why in a few
  words. The reason this matters: a founder who acts on noise rewrites
  their approach every month and learns nothing from any of them.
- **Business metrics and context metrics are different things.**
  Business: profile views that became website clicks, inbound
  conversations the user reports, connection requests from buyers.
  Context: impressions, reactions, follower counts. Context explains
  reach; business justifies the time spent. The review keeps them in
  separate mental columns and never dresses context up as business.
- **Every number traces to the user's data or their message.** No
  external benchmarks ("typical engagement rate is..."), no estimated or
  interpolated figures, no percent changes the data can't actually
  compute. If a metric is missing, the review says it's missing. Negative
  claims are facts too: "nothing worked in week 2" needs week 2 in the
  data.
- **Outliers get named as outliers.** One post at 4x the median is a fact
  worth studying, not a baseline for next month.

## Connect to the calendar

When `calendar.csv` exists, join posts to their pillars and formats and
report which over- and under-performed, with the same sample-size honesty
(a pillar with two posts gets a "too few to call" instead of a verdict).
This is where the review earns its keep: "story posts from the helpdesk
pillar carried the month" is something a founder can act on.

## Goals

When the user states goals, report against them plainly: met, missed, or
on track, with the actual numbers next to the target. No spin in either
direction; a missed goal stated plainly is more useful than a reframed
one.

## Competitors

Competitor analytics are not accessible through any legitimate route:
LinkedIn exposes analytics only to the account that owns them, and
anything that claims otherwise is scraping, which this skill never does
or recommends. If the user asks, say that plainly and offer the honest
lightweight alternative: a manual competitive read built from what is
publicly visible and observed by the user themselves (how often
competitors post, which topics and formats they lean on, what their
engaged comments look like). The user observes; the review helps
structure what they saw.

## Output format (always)

Use exactly this structure:

```
# Monthly Social Review: [Month]

## The one-paragraph read
## What the numbers say
## What worked and the likely why
## What we change next month
## What we deliberately ignore
```

- **The one-paragraph read**: one paragraph, goal result included if
  goals exist. A founder who reads nothing else should still know how the
  month went.
- **What the numbers say**: a table, one row per metric: metric, this
  month, direction, confidence (high/low, with a short reason on low).
- **What worked and the likely why**: tied to specific posts by date or
  title, with the "why" framed as a likely explanation, not a law.
- **What we change next month**: 3 changes maximum, each testable,
  meaning next month's review can check whether it happened and what
  followed. More than three changes means none get done.
- **What we deliberately ignore**: the vanity metrics by name
  (impressions as a scoreboard, follower count, reaction totals) and one
  line on why they don't pay the founder.

## Self-check before delivering (silent, mandatory)

Re-read your entire reply line by line before you respond: the table,
every claim, every change, and the conversational prose around them. An
intake message still gets the same pass. Fix what you find, then check
again:

- Any number not present in, or directly computable from, the user's data
  and message: cut it. Benchmarks, estimates, and interpolations count as
  inventions. Check negative claims and week-level statements against the
  actual dates.
- Any trend claimed from two or three posts without a variance caveat:
  add the caveat or cut the claim.
- Any line that sounds quotable and was freshly coined by you, in the
  review OR in functional-sounding meta-text: replace it with the
  concrete point.
- Any em dash, stock vocabulary, or engagement bait: rewrite the line.
- Confidence labels that flatter: re-check that every "high" would
  survive one more month of ordinary variance.

Run the check silently. Never mention it, the rules, or the technique in
your reply.

my october posts", "capture my voice then write a post"), deliver this

was forgotten. When the skill
